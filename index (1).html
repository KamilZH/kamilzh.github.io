Method

How the number is built

Four inputs, each normalised against its trailing 52-week median so that 50 means "an ordinary week", then weighted and summed.

  1. Median anchorage dwell, container vessels over 5,000 TEU — 40%
  2. Count of vessels at anchor inside the coverage box — 25%
  3. Median time alongside, berth to departure — 20%
  4. Arrivals over the trailing seven days — 15%

Each score is 50 × (current ÷ baseline), capped at 100. Dwell and vessel count carry most of the weight because they respond first when the port slows; berth time and arrival volume move later and confirm.

40–60Normal
60–80Elevated
80–100Severe

What is live and what is not

The plot is real. Positions, speeds and counts come straight off AIS the moment a key is connected, and a vessel is counted at anchor when it reports under half a knot.

The index itself is not live yet, and saying otherwise would be dishonest. Weekly medians and dwell need a recorder running around the clock for a year before there is a baseline to compare against. A browser tab only sees what happens while it is open, so the four inputs stay hand-entered until that collector exists.

Known limits

  • AIS drops out near the breakwater; short calls can be missed entirely.
  • Anchoring is not always congestion — vessels wait on cargo readiness, bunkers and crew changes too.
  • Terrestrial AIS coverage is patchy. Counts are a floor, not a census.

The live connection

AISStream publishes global AIS over one websocket. This page opens it directly from the browser, subscribes to a box drawn around Jebel Ali, and plots every position report it receives.

Turning this into a real index

The missing piece is persistence. Run the same subscription on a small always-on process, write every position report to a database, and compute the weekly medians from that instead of from a browser tab.

  1. Run the collector on anything that stays awake — a Raspberry Pi is enough.
  2. Record MMSI, position, speed and timestamp. One row per report.
  3. Derive dwell from the gap between a vessel going stationary and moving again.
  4. Roll up weekly. After a year you have baselines and the index computes itself.

Publishing rhythm

  • Pull the rollup every Monday morning Gulf time.
  • Post the reading, the direction, and one sentence on why.
  • Keep the archive public — the back series is the asset, not any single week.

About

Compiled by Kamil Zakir Hussain, who is completing an MSc in Supply Chain Management at Boston University. Jebel Ali moves more containers than any port between Singapore and Rotterdam, and yet there is no public measure of how long ships wait to get into it. This is an attempt at one.

The method is open on this page so anyone can argue with it. Corrections and better ideas are welcome.
